Awin Global · 2023–2025 · Case Study

From Last-Click to Last-Cause

How a causal measurement system revealed that Awin's brand awareness campaigns were adding volume but destroying lead quality, and how proving that finding twice, after leadership initially pushed back, restructured ~€500K in quarterly marketing budget toward channels with genuine incremental lift.

Geo-Lift HoldoutsIncrementality TestingCUPED Variance ReductionBudget Optimisation
My role
Data Specialist, Growth & Marketing Measurement. Owned design, analysis and the rollout conversation.
Stack
BigQuery · dbt · Looker · Python · Geo-lift + CUPED
Business problem
~€500K per quarter allocated across channels on last-click signals nobody had tested.
Outcome
Brand lift ≈ 0 net qualified opportunities. ~€140K per quarter moved to referral and performance search; wasted spend −20%.
01, The Business Problem

The campaigns looked like they were working.

The question was whether they were causing anything.

Awin's internal marketing team was running campaigns across Google Search, referral programmes, and paid channels with a single goal: to drive new advertiser opportunities onto the platform and convert them into active clients. The baseline was strong, we were converting 64% of inbound opportunities across organic and direct channels. Campaign activity appeared to be adding to that pipeline, and on last-click attribution, the numbers looked healthy across the board.

But last-click has a structural blind spot. It tells you what touchpoint preceded a conversion, not what caused it. A prospect who had already decided to evaluate Awin, who had already been through an initial sales conversation, who was already deep in the funnel, they'd still get credited to whichever campaign they happened to click last. The question the data didn't answer: how many of these opportunities would have arrived and converted without any campaign contact at all?

"We were reporting campaign performance based on what came last, not what caused the decision. For a platform built on performance marketing principles, measuring our own acquisition on last-click was a contradiction we needed to resolve."

With approximately €500K in quarterly marketing budget being allocated across channels based on these signals, the cost of getting this wrong was real. Brand awareness campaigns were consuming a significant share of the budget, and no one had yet tested whether the conversion credit they were claiming had been earned.

02, Why the Obvious Approach Wouldn't Work

Pausing a channel for a week tells you nothing in a B2B sales cycle.

The instinct in most marketing teams is to turn off a channel and watch what happens. In a B2B platform context where the advertiser sales cycle runs weeks to months, a short pause produces noise, not signal. Any effect would be buried in pipeline lag, seasonal variation, and the natural rhythm of a long conversion funnel. You need holdouts calibrated to the sales cycle length, not just the campaign flight window.

There was also an organisational constraint that mattered as much as the technical one. The brand awareness campaigns had strong internal advocates with years of reported performance data behind them. Any measurement approach that could be dismissed as too short, too narrow, or methodologically weak would simply get set aside. We needed a design that was both causally rigorous and institutionally defensible, because the results were going to face real scrutiny.

Awin operates across 10+ markets with different competitive dynamics, different sales cycle lengths, and different channel compositions. A single global measurement wouldn't generalise, we needed market-level incrementality estimates that could be aggregated reliably and interrogated market by market if challenged.

03, What I Designed

A geo-lift framework built around the actual sales cycle, not the campaign window.

  • 1.
    Geo-lift holdouts across GSA marketsRandomised geographic holdout experiments across the GSA region, splitting comparable markets into treatment (campaigns running) and control (campaigns suppressed) groups. Markets were matched on pre-period opportunity conversion volume and seasonality profiles to ensure structural similarity before any experiment started. Each holdout ran for 4–6 weeks, long enough to capture a real conversion signal across the sales cycle, short enough to limit pipeline risk in suppressed markets.
  • 2.
    Isolating campaign type as the treatment variableRather than measuring "all campaigns" as a single block, I designed the holdouts to isolate brand awareness creative vs performance-focused creative as separate treatment conditions. This was the design decision that produced the most actionable finding: the incrementality gap wasn't uniform, it was concentrated almost entirely in brand awareness.
  • 3.
    CUPED variance reduction for sensitivityMarket-level experiments have inherently fewer statistical units than user-level tests. I applied CUPED using pre-period opportunity conversion metrics as covariates to reduce residual variance, meaningfully increasing the sensitivity of each experiment within the 4–6 week window, without extending holdouts to the point of creating unacceptable sales pipeline risk in suppressed markets.
  • 4.
    Production measurement platform in BigQuery, dbt, and LookerAutomated BigQuery pipelines pulling campaign, GTM tracking, and CRM conversion data; 12+ dbt models transforming raw events into standardised KPIs, opportunity CAC, conversion rate by source, payback period; and Looker dashboards giving marketing and product teams real-time self-serve access across markets. Reporting time dropped from a 2-day manual process to same-day. KPI definitions standardised across four growth teams who had previously been calculating them inconsistently.

The brand wasn't doing anything. It was adding volume and subtracting quality.

The first round of holdout experiments produced a finding more nuanced than a simple "brand doesn't work" conclusion, and more commercially significant because of it. When brand awareness campaigns were suppressed in control markets, inbound opportunity volume dropped approximately 12% in weeks 1–2. The channel was generating real volume.

But two other findings changed the interpretation entirely. First, the quality gap: brand-sourced leads were converting at approximately ~51%, a full 20 percentage points below the 64% baseline conversion rate from organic and direct opportunities. The campaigns were filling the top of the funnel with prospects who were structurally less likely to become clients.

The nuance that changed everything: Brand added ~12% volume but those leads converted at ~51% vs 64% for organic. Multiply volume × quality and net qualified opportunity output was close to zero. At ~€200K/quarter, that math mattered.

Second, and more conclusively, the recovery pattern. By weeks 4–6 of the holdout, the 12% volume drop in control markets had fully recovered. Organic and direct demand absorbed the gap naturally, without any campaign intervention. The volume brand awareness was generating wasn't incremental demand, it was latent demand arriving slightly earlier than it would have otherwise.

The net arithmetic: brand campaigns added ~12% volume at ~51% conversion vs a 64% organic baseline. Once quality-adjusted, the incremental output of qualified opportunities was close to zero, while consuming the largest single share of the quarterly budget.

05, The Outcome

Leadership pushed back. We ran it again. The finding held.

The initial results met resistance. Brand awareness had strong internal advocates, and the finding, that the channel with the largest budget share had near-zero net qualified impact, was an uncomfortable one. Rather than relitigate the methodology, we reran the geo-lift holdouts with a second wave of experiments, extending the measurement window and adding market pairs to increase statistical confidence.

The finding replicated. The quality gap held at ~20 percentage points. The recovery curve appeared again. With two rounds of evidence pointing to the same conclusion, the conversation shifted from "is this right?" to "what do we do about it?" The result was a full reallocation of the ~€500K quarterly budget, total spend held flat, allocation rebuilt around channels with validated incremental lift and conversion quality at or above the 64% organic baseline.

Axel Springer National Media & Tech · 2022 · Case Study

22% Was a Lie. 15% Changed the Roadmap.

How auditing event-tracking pipelines across BILD and WELT revealed that the Registration→Trial conversion rate was being reported at 22% when the real number was 15%, a 47% inflation hiding the most significant leak in the subscription funnel.

Funnel AnalysisExperimentation DesignEvent Tracking AuditData Quality
My role
Business Analyst. Ran the audit, redesigned the testing methodology across teams.
Stack
Snowflake · GA4 · Adobe Analytics · Power analysis
Business problem
A 22% registration-to-trial rate was setting roadmap priorities. The tracking behind it had never been audited.
Outcome
True rate 15%, a 47% inflation. Experiment reliability +20% across 5 analytics teams.
01, The Business Problem

The data looked right. That was the problem.

Axel Springer's digital properties, BILD and WELT, were making decisions every week based on funnel analytics and A/B test results. The tracking infrastructure and experimentation methodology underpinning those decisions had never been properly audited.

Duplicate event fires. Misfired tracking triggers. Tests were stopped the moment they crossed a significance threshold. Across five analytics teams, these weren't isolated incidents, they were systemic. And because nobody had surfaced them, the teams had no reason to question the numbers they were acting on.

"The most dangerous measurement failure isn't one that produces obviously wrong answers, it's one that produces plausible wrong answers. Numbers that look right, behave right, and get used confidently."

A product team with 22% showing in their dashboard has no reason to prioritise that step. A product team with the real number, 15%, has an immediate roadmap conversation.

02, What Was Actually Broken

Two failure modes. One shared root cause.

The tracking failure. Misfired triggers were recording trial start events that hadn't actually been completed, inflating the apparent conversion rate at Registration→Trial. Duplicate event fires were double-counting certain user actions. All five analytics teams were working on data that had never been validated against source of truth.

The second failure was methodological. Teams were stopping A/B tests early as soon as results crossed a significance threshold rather than running to their pre-specified sample size. Stopping at first significance inflates false positive rates dramatically.

"The teams weren't being careless, they were being responsive. An underpowered positive result and a real positive result look identical in a dashboard. The only thing separating them is whether the test ran long enough."

03, What I Built

Fix the foundation. Then trust everything built on top of it.

  • 1.
    Full event-tracking audit across BILD and WELTSystematically mapped every tracked event against its intended trigger, identified duplicate fires, located misfired triggers, and cross-referenced event counts against server-side conversion data across all five analytics teams.
  • 2.
    Automated data quality checks to prevent recurrenceImplemented automated quality checks validating event counts and alerting teams to tracking discrepancies before they contaminated live experiments. Shifted from reactive discovery to proactive detection at the point of data ingestion.
  • 3.
    Rebuilt experimentation methodology across five teamsMandatory sample size calculations before experiment launch, holdout controls, statistical power analysis setting minimum detectable effect sizes, and hard stopping rules. A feature test that had previously been called positive after 1 week and 800 users now required 5 weeks and 8,000 users.
  • 4.
    Multi-platform funnel dashboards on clean dataWith the tracking layer validated, built funnel dashboards surfacing true conversion rates at each step. The Registration→Trial drop-off became visible at its real scale for the first time.

22% reported. 15% real. A 47% inflation hiding the most important leak.

06, What I'd Do Differently

Honest reflection.

The tracking audit found the problems reactively. If I were starting again, I'd run a full tracking audit as the first deliverable of any analytics engagement, before touching any experiment data, before building any dashboard.

The early-stopping problem was cultural as much as technical. Adding hard stopping rules was the right intervention, but in retrospect I'd have paired it with a concrete demonstration: here are the tests you called positive in the last six months, here is the probability that each one was noise. Understanding changes behaviour permanently.

Gorillas Operations · 2022–2023 · Case Study

€1 of discount, €1 of revenue, or not?

How causal uplift modelling and CLV-based segmentation revealed that ~28% of Gorillas' promotional spend in Germany and the Netherlands was being absorbed by customers who would have ordered anyway, and how two counterintuitive pricing findings reshaped how every promotional euro was targeted.

Uplift ModellingCLV SegmentationPricing StrategyCausal Inference
My role
Commercial Operations & Growth Analyst. Built the models and the segment targeting logic.
Stack
Python · T-learner / causal forests · BigQuery · CLV cohorts
Business problem
Blanket discounting across all customers, with no way to tell who would have ordered anyway.
Outcome
~€33K per quarter of promotional waste recovered. Campaign ROI +15%, promo waste −18% across DE and NL.
01, The Business Problem

In quick-commerce, everyone receives a discount. That was the problem.

Gorillas was operating in one of the most aggressively promotional environments in European e-commerce, a market where customer acquisition cost was high, margins were razor-thin, and the competitive pressure to discount was constant. The promotional budget across German and Dutch markets was running at approximately €120K per quarter. Every customer, new, returning, loyal, or lapsed, was receiving similar promotional offers, regardless of whether the discount was actually changing their behaviour.

A high redemption rate from a loyal customer who was going to order anyway isn't a marketing success, it's a margin reduction on a sale you already owned. The question nobody was asking rigorously: for how many of our customers is this discount actually causing an order that wouldn't have happened otherwise?

"In a startup burning cash to win market share, every promotional euro needs to be creating incremental revenue, not subsidising behaviour that was already going to happen. The aggregate numbers couldn't tell us which was which."

02, The Real Obstacle Wasn't the Model

Before any insight could exist, the data had to.

Gorillas had no single data warehouse. Customer records, order history, promotional redemption data, and pricing data lived in separate systems across markets, each city-level operation with its own schema, its own update cadence, its own level of completeness. The first month of this project wasn't modelling. It was data archaeology: tracing customer identifiers across systems, reconciling promotional exposure flags, and building the pipelines that made a unified analytical layer possible.

"Measurement infrastructure is not a precondition for analytical work, it is the analytical work, at least until it exists. In an environment without a warehouse, the data scientist who can build pipelines and run models is worth more than one who can only do the latter."

03, What I Built

Segment first. Model response. Then price by market.

  • 1.
    Data unification pipeline across DE, NL, and UKBuilt consolidation pipelines standardising customer identifiers, order cadence metrics, and promotional exposure flags across city-level data sources. Germany and the Netherlands became the primary analytical markets.
  • 2.
    CLV-based customer segmentation using cohort analysisUsing cohort-based LTV models on the unified behavioural data, I identified three structurally distinct customer types defined by their behavioural relationship with pricing and promotions. Habitual orderers were worth approximately 3× a deal-hunter at 90 days.
  • 3.
    T-learner uplift models to separate response from coincidenceDeployed T-learner causal uplift models, training separate response models on treated and control populations, then computing individual treatment effects. The output was a propensity score per customer, estimating whether a promotion would cause an incremental order or simply be redeemed on a sale that was already happening.
  • 4.
    Pricing elasticity modelling across four levers and three marketsModelled price elasticity across delivery fee changes, minimum order value thresholds, product-level price changes, and promotional discount depth. Germany and the Netherlands showed near-identical elasticity patterns. The UK diverged significantly, requiring a separate model.

05, What We Found

Bigger discounts didn't buy proportionally more orders. And €2 off delivery beats both.

Approximately 28% of customers in Germany and the Netherlands showed near-zero treatment effect, promotions had no meaningful causal impact on their ordering behaviour. At ~€120K quarterly spend, roughly €33K per quarter was being absorbed by customers whose behaviour wasn't changing.

A 20% discount drove almost the same incremental order volume as a 30% discount across promotion-responsive segments. The last 10 percentage points of discount depth were buying almost nothing, pure margin cost with no behavioural return.

"€2 off the delivery fee outperformed a 20% basket discount in Germany on incremental order conversion. Delivery fee reduction removes a binary barrier, 'do I pay anything to receive this order?', while basket discounts adjust a continuous price variable. One changes the decision frame. The other changes a number within it."

06, What Changed

Three segments. Three strategies. One budget that worked harder.

Habitual orderers were shifted out of standard promotional flows and replaced with retention-focused communications. Deal-hunters received promotions capped at 20% depth rather than 30%, recovering margin with no meaningful reduction in incremental order volume. Lapsed users received personalised win-back sequences with expectations reset around their likely post-reactivation value.

07, What I'd Do Differently

Honest reflection.

The data fragmentation problem was the constraint that shaped everything else, and I accepted it as a given rather than treating it as the first thing to solve. If I were starting again, I would push for a centralised warehouse as a project prerequisite, not an ongoing workaround.

The lapsed user segment deserved its own dedicated controlled experiment. The surface signal (high reactivation rate) and the underlying reality (low post-reactivation CLV) took too long to reconcile. Building the 90-day CLV measurement into the reactivation campaign logic from day one would have changed how the segment was prioritised and funded.

01, The Brief

Ninety six hours, two questions, one CMO

Sunday Natural, a Berlin-based direct-to-consumer supplements brand, sent me two questions with a four day clock attached.

The first: influencer spend reports a 4.5× return, credited off voucher redemptions. The codes leak to deal aggregator sites within hours of a post going live. The CMO suspects the number is fiction. How would you find the real one?

The second: the loyalty programme is six months old. Here is a customer level dataset. Is it working?

On the surface these are unrelated. A paid media problem and a retention problem. Underneath, it is the same failure printed twice. In both cases someone had computed a number correctly, the number looked excellent, and nobody in the building could say what would have happened anyway.

That gap, between what you observed and the world where you did nothing, is the whole job. What follows is my attempt to estimate it twice under a deadline, and then say plainly which parts of it I would put budget behind.

02, Why the Obvious Approach Fails

Fix the attribution. Compare the members. Report the numbers.

Both questions have an obvious answer that takes an afternoon, and both obvious answers are wrong in the same way.

For influencer, a better attribution model cannot help. The instinct is to move off last-touch voucher credit onto something more sophisticated. It will not work, because the problem is not how credit is assigned. Once a code reaches a deal aggregator, redemption stops being evidence that anyone saw the creator. No model recovers exposure from a signal that no longer carries it. You need a different signal, which means an experiment.

For loyalty, the comparison is the bias. Members generated 2.94× the revenue of non-members. Enrolment was opt in at checkout. So the comparison measures who chose to join, and the people most likely to opt into a loyalty scheme are the people who were already loyal. Any analysis that starts after launch inherits that problem no matter how carefully it is done.

And six months is enough to establish one thing, not three. With 560 customers the basket effect is tight enough to defend and the revenue effect is not. The confidence interval decides which is which, and reporting a point estimate without it is how a programme gets funded on a p value of 0.12.

So neither question was really about analysis. One needed an experiment designed, and the other needed the six months before launch looked at first.

03, Question One

The signal was broken, not the model

Nothing was wrong with the attribution logic. A code is redeemed, the code belongs to a creator, the creator gets the revenue. Clean, auditable, and completely wrong the moment that code stops being a private handshake between a creator and their audience.

Once a code is public, redemption proves only that the customer found a discount. It has stopped being evidence of exposure.

Three things follow from that, and only the first is about the ROAS number itself.

Budget moves the wrong way, every quarter. Influencer gets compared against channels that are not inflated in the same manner, so the comparison is rigged before the planning meeting starts.

Creator rankings invert. The creators whose codes spread fastest look like the strongest performers, and spread is the exact mechanism corrupting the measurement. The leaderboard rewards the leak.

Margin drains quietly. Customers who would have paid full price get handed a discount by a website the brand never chose to advertise on.

Before designing any test I would spend one day on arithmetic. Referrer data already records how each customer arrived: creator link, aggregator site, or a bare code search. That split alone tells you how much of the 4.5× deserves any trust at all.

04, The Design

Stop chasing the code. Switch off the channel.

A geo holdout walks around the leak instead of trying to fix it. You stop tracing individual codes and switch influencer activity off in a random set of regions. Codes can leak all they like, because now they leak on both sides of the comparison. What you measure is total regional revenue.

Two digit postcode regions as the unit, stratified on pre-period revenue, orders and average basket so the groups start from the same place. Six to eight weeks, sized off the minimum detectable effect. Difference in differences on revenue with market clustered standard errors, and CUPED on historical regional revenue to cut the variance. If pre-trends fail to hold, synthetic control with placebo tests on untreated markets.

08, The Call

Do not scale it. Make it provable.

A 2.94× headline invites you to pour budget in. A null result invites you to shut it down. Both are overreactions to an underpowered read on a group that selected itself, and both are avoidable with one product decision.

Hold spend flat

Nothing here justifies expanding investment. Nothing justifies cutting a programme with a real, established basket size effect either. Flat is the position the evidence actually supports.

Randomise the next cohort

Randomly assign eligible customers to the programme or a control at the point of offer. That removes the self selection problem permanently. It is a product change, not an analytics project, and it costs one sprint.

Test the mechanism

Basket size is the one thing that works, so find out why. Separate the points mechanic from member only offers, so that scaling means scaling the half that actually moves the basket.

in the room

"Give me one product change, randomise the next intake, and in six months I can tell you what this programme is worth instead of what it correlates with."

Both questions ended in the same place, and that is the part I would want a hiring team to notice. A channel measurement problem and a retention evaluation collapsed into one discipline: find the comparison group, size the effect honestly, separate what is established from what is merely suggested, and attach a decision rule before anyone has a stake in the answer.

The 4.5× and the 2.94× were never lies. They were confident answers to questions nobody had asked carefully enough.

09, What I'd Do Differently

Honest reflection.

The break-even slide rests on two numbers I made up. A 40% gross margin and a programme cost per member, neither of which I had. €7.48 of incremental margin is arithmetic on an assumption, and it went onto a slide looking like a finding. The business has both figures sitting in finance, and asking for them was an email I did not send because I was four days into a four day window. If the answer flips inside a plausible range, and it does, then getting the real inputs matters more than refining the estimate.

The basket-size result is my strongest finding and I buried its weakness. It is estimated on the 280 customers who ordered in both windows, and the programme plausibly affects whether someone orders at all, which makes that sample a post-treatment selection rather than a clean one. So €19.12 is the effect among purchasers, not the effect on members. I put that in a caveat. It belonged in the main analysis, next to the number.

I also reported the retention gap as a raw eight point difference in lapse rate. Recomputing from the dataset, the raw gap is closer to 29 points and roughly 7 survive adjustment. I had quoted an adjusted figure and labelled it raw. It does not change the conclusion, which is that retention is not established either way, but mislabelling which number is which is exactly the error the rest of this analysis is about.

What I would keep is the order of operations. Looking at the six months before launch before looking at the result is what turned a flattering 2.94× into a real finding. It cost twenty minutes and it was the whole analysis.

01, The Brief

Define value, then decide where the money goes

ParshipMeet Group, which operates Parship, ElitePartner and a portfolio of dating and matchmaking brands, ran four acquisition channels over six weeks: Meta, Google, Affiliate and Organic. €781,000 of spend, 88,650 registrations, and a funnel that goes registration to trial-free to order to net paid revenue after withdrawals.

Two questions. First, how should the business define lifetime value, and what are the limits of that definition. Second, is the current budget allocation justified.

The trap in the first question is that it sounds theoretical. It is not. Whatever definition you pick determines the answer to the second question, and the definition you pick is constrained by the data you actually have. Which, here, did not include a single renewal.

Before any of that I found two errors in the source and corrected them. One transcription error in Organic week two, and one collapse in Google week four that I judged to be a tracking failure rather than a performance event. That decision alone moves Google's six week ROI from 83.3% to 94.1%, which is enough to change a budget recommendation, so it belongs in the open rather than in a footnote.

02, Why the Obvious Approach Fails

Rank the channels by return, cut the worst, scale the best

That is what the ROI column invites you to do, and on these numbers it is a short conversation. Meta returns 53.55% and takes 48% of the budget. Organic returns 709% on 3.8%. Move the money.

Three things stop that being an answer.

ROI here is a first-payment number and the product is a subscription. A channel can lose money on the first payment and still be the best investment in the portfolio, if the people it brings stay. Ranking on first-payment return answers a question nobody asked.

The variable that decides it is not in the dataset. There are zero renewal observations across all 24 rows. Not partial, not noisy, absent. So every lifetime figure anyone produces from this data is an assumption wearing a number's clothing, including mine.

Organic is not a lever you can pull. The 709% return is the most eye-catching figure in the deck and the least actionable, because the €5,000 a week behind it is tracking tooling rather than media. You cannot buy more organic by putting a bigger number in the cell.

What you can do, and what this case turned into, is invert the model. Instead of guessing what each channel is worth, work out what each channel needs to be true, then hand that claim to the team who can verify it.

03, Defining Value

One formula, one unknown, no fixed multipliers

The tempting definitions both fail. Net paid revenue on the first payment counts one transaction and stops, which understates any channel whose users stay. A fixed multiplier, revenue times three or times five, assumes every channel renews identically, which is precisely the assumption the question is asking you to test.

So the model is a geometric series. If a user renews with probability r, the expected number of renewals is r / (1 - r), and total value including the first payment is:

LTV = NPR per order ÷ (1 − r) One input. It updates the moment CRM delivers real renewal rates by channel.

Why a geometric series and not something else

Four reasons, in the order they mattered.

It matches how a subscription actually behaves. A user who renews once faces the same decision again at the next renewal. If that decision has probability r each time, the expected number of renewals is r + r² + r³ ..., which sums to r / (1 - r). Add the first payment and total expected payments is 1 / (1 - r). At 35% renewal that is 1.54 payments; at 50% it is exactly 2. The series is not a modelling flourish, it is the arithmetic of a decision that repeats.

It has exactly one unknown, and that unknown is somebody's job. Revenue per order is measured. Cost per order is measured. The renewal rate is not, and it sits in the CRM. A model with one clearly labelled gap is far more useful than one with a plausible number quietly baked in where the gap used to be, because the first can be closed by an email and the second cannot be closed at all.

It stays correct when the business changes. A fixed multiplier is a snapshot of a plan mix. The moment the 24 month plan is phased out, every multiplier in the deck is stale and nobody notices. Here, when renewal rates move, the model moves with them and the recommendation updates itself.

It inverts cleanly. Because there is one unknown, you can solve for it rather than guess it, which is what section 03 does. A model with three assumed inputs cannot be inverted into a single testable question.

What it assumes, stated plainly

Four assumptions, all of which are wrong at the margins and none of which I would hide.

Renewal probability is constant across periods. Real churn is front-loaded, so a single r overstates value for a channel whose users leave early and understates it for one whose survivors are durable. Cohort renewals at 30, 60 and 90 days would let this be replaced with a proper survival curve.

A renewal is worth what the first payment was worth. Renewals may be discounted, upgraded or priced on a different plan. Nothing in this dataset says either way.

Revenue, not margin, and undiscounted. There is no servicing cost and no time value of money in here. Both push true value down, so every LTV figure on this page is an upper bound on economic value even before the renewal assumption is questioned.

Renewal rates differ by channel. That is the entire premise. If they do not, the model still works and the ranking simply collapses back to first-payment ROI, which is section 05's point.

What sits inside the numerator

Revenue per order is not a single quantity, and taking it apart changes how two channels read. It decomposes exactly into the subscription price and the share of buyers who do not withdraw.

04, The Identity

The answer was already in the ROI column

Here is the part I did not expect. Break-even renewal is the rate at which lifetime value equals what you paid to acquire the user. Set LTV equal to CAC and solve:

r* = 1 − (NPR per order ÷ CAC) = 1 − ROI Because NPR per order divided by cost per order is just revenue over cost.

The break-even renewal rate is one minus the first-payment ROI. Exactly. No approximation, no CRM extract, no waiting six months. Every business already computing ROI is already computing this and reporting it in a form that hides what it means.

Meta's ROI of 53.55% is not merely a weak number. It is a statement that 46.4% of Meta's users must renew before the channel returns its acquisition cost. Suddenly the same figure is a testable claim about user behaviour rather than a line on a dashboard.

Try to make Meta win. It is harder than it looks. Because efficiency reduces to ROI over one minus r, and Meta's first-payment ROI is 43% lower than Google's, Meta has to renew dramatically better to compensate. At Google's assumed 50%, Meta needs 71.5%. There is no plausible world in which paid social users renew at seven in ten while paid search users renew at five.

That is the most useful output of the whole exercise. Not a number, but a demonstration that the recommendation is robust to the thing we do not know. I do not need renewal data to reduce Meta and scale Google. I would need it to say by exactly how much.

Google's efficiency here uses its 94.06% ROI with week four excluded, consistent with the exclusion argued in section 06. My submitted deck showed 1.67x for Google, computed on the ROI including that week. Excluding it strengthens the case rather than weakening it, which is exactly why the exclusion needed to be argued in the open rather than applied quietly.

07, Three Problems

The other three channels are not one story

It would be neat to say the portfolio has a Meta problem. It does not. It has three different problems that happen to sit in the same table, and each needs a different owner.

Google
A reliable engine with one unexplained hole

Trial to order sits at 29 to 30% in five of six weeks, roughly 1.7 times Meta's rate, on the lowest break-even threshold in the portfolio at 16.7%.

Week four is the exception: paid conversion collapsed to 10% while registrations and trial signups held flat. A demand collapse does not spare the two steps above it. I read that as tracking failure, excluded it, and flagged it for engineering rather than quietly averaging it away.

Affiliate
The best unit economics and a shrinking pool

€90 net revenue per order, the highest of any paid channel, and above 100% ROI in every single week. It pays for itself on the first purchase. The decomposition in section 02 shows why: same €100 price as Meta and Organic, but only 10% of buyers withdraw, the best rate in the portfolio.

And registrations fell 22.5% across six weeks on flat spend, which is a supply problem, not a performance problem. The aggregate could be two strong partners carrying a weak tail, so cutting before a partner-level breakdown risks removing the best traffic in the portfolio.

Organic
Not a lever, a signal

€12 to acquire a user against Meta's €148. Same product, same subscription, twelve times cheaper, with registrations up 16% over six weeks and no incremental spend behind them.

The trap is treating a 709% return as a budget line to increase. The €5,000 a week is tooling, not media. You cannot buy more of this directly, which is why the recommendation is SEO and brand investment rather than a bigger number in the same cell.

Two corrections to my own submission. I described Google as converting at twice Meta's rate; recomputed it is 1.67 times on trial to order and 1.70 times on registration to trial, still decisive and worth stating accurately. And I attributed Affiliate's revenue advantage to a skew toward longer plans, which the price decomposition disproves. The advantage is withdrawal rate, not plan length.

08, What Changes My Mind

Five gaps, each with an owner and a consequence

A missing data slide is usually where a deck goes to apologise. This one is meant to be actionable, so every row names who has the data and which specific recommendation flips when it arrives.

Affiliate is the row I would push hardest after renewals, for a reason the price decomposition sharpened rather than settled. Affiliate traffic is widely assumed to be low quality, and this data shows it selling at the same €100 as everyone else with the lowest cancellation rate in the portfolio. Users who arrive through partners are the least likely to regret the purchase. If that holds at partner level, a long-standing assumption inside the business is wrong and the channel is being underfunded because of it.

A limit worth stating about the exercise itself: this is 24 rows over six weeks, and three of the four channels have conversion rates that barely move week to week. Organic is identical to the decimal in all six weeks. So the trends I can genuinely read are Meta's spend response and Affiliate's registration decline. Everything else is a level, not a trend, and I have treated it that way.

09, The Call

Three decisions, one of which is a request

What I would ask leadership to agree in the meeting, and what happens next in each case.

Cap Meta at €55,000 a week

Quality decayed above that level and recovered below it. Redirect €10,000 to €15,000 weekly to Google. Then run Meta at €55,000 against €70,000 with fresh creative, to separate a spend ceiling from creative fatigue rather than assuming.

Scale Google to €60,000 to €65,000

Stable 30% paid conversion across five valid weeks and the lowest break-even requirement in the portfolio at 16.7%. Increase incrementally with weekly conversion as the gate, and diagnose week four before the first increase, not after.

Commission renewal analysis in three weeks

This is the request, not a recommendation. Cohort renewals at 30, 60 and 90 days, segmented by channel and plan length. That single dataset either confirms everything above or rewrites it.

What I would want a hiring team to take from this is the sequencing. The framework came before the numbers, so that what counts as good performance was defined while I still had no stake in the answer. The two data errors were found and disclosed before any analysis ran. And the one variable that decides everything was isolated, named and handed to the team that owns it, rather than being replaced with a confident assumption and forgotten.

in the room

"I can tell you to move money out of Meta today and I can show you that holds under any renewal rate you find plausible. What I cannot tell you yet is what Meta is actually worth. Give me three weeks of CRM data and that stops being a guess."

10, What I'd Do Differently

Honest reflection.

I put assumed renewal rates in a table. They were labelled as estimates, the note under the table said so plainly, and it did not matter. The moment a number sits in a cell marked LTV, somebody quotes it in a meeting without the label. If I ran this again I would lead with the break-even requirement, which is measured, and move the assumed-LTV table into an appendix where it belongs. The finding was never that Meta is worth €122. It was that Meta needs 46.4% of its users to renew.

I wrote that Google converts at twice Meta's rate. It is 1.67 times on trial to order and 1.70 on registration to trial. Both are decisive, and rounding 1.67 up to two bought me nothing while giving anyone who checks a reason to question the rest. That is a bad trade and I made it for the sake of a cleaner sentence.

I hypothesised that Affiliate's €90 revenue per order came from users buying longer plans, and flagged it as needing confirmation rather than testing it. Decomposing revenue per order into price and withdrawal rate takes about ten minutes and disproves it: Affiliate sells at the same €100 as everyone except Google, and its whole advantage is a 10% cancellation rate. I had the data and reached for a hypothesis instead.

What I would keep is the inversion. Computing break-even renewal from numbers already on hand, instead of waiting on a CRM extract, is the move that turned a four day exercise into something a leadership team could act on immediately. It cost nothing and it reframed the entire budget conversation.

How the numbers were built

Channel aggregates, six weeks

  • Google: €266,000 (34.1%), 20,700 registrations, 3,259 orders, €221,683. ROI 83.34% including week four and 94.06% excluding it, CAC €81.62, revenue per order €68.02, break-even renewal 16.7%.
  • Affiliate: €110,000 (14.1%), 10,400 registrations, 1,237 orders, €111,330. ROI 101.21%, CAC €88.92, revenue per order €90.00, break-even renewal 0%.
  • Organic: €30,000 (3.8%), 16,250 registrations, 2,503 orders, €212,755. ROI 709.18%, CAC €11.99, revenue per order €85.00, break-even renewal 0%.
  • All figures recomputed from the raw weekly table rather than read from the summary sheet, and reconciled against it.
The LTV model
  • LTV = (NPR ÷ orders) ÷ (1 − r), where r is the per-period renewal probability. Expected renewals under a constant r are r/(1 − r), so total expected payments including the first is 1/(1 − r). Verified: at r = 0.35 that is 1.538 payments, at r = 0.50 exactly 2.
  • Stated assumptions: renewal probability constant across periods (real churn is front-loaded, so this overstates value for early-leaving cohorts); renewals worth the same as the first payment; revenue rather than margin, undiscounted, making every figure an upper bound; and renewal rates genuinely differing by channel, which is the model's premise rather than its conclusion.
  • Revenue per order decomposes exactly as price × (1 − withdrawal rate). Implied price is constant per channel to the cent across all six weeks: €100.00 for Meta, Affiliate and Organic, €80.03 for Google. Retention after withdrawal is 90.0%, 85.0%, 79.1% and 85.0% respectively.
  • Break-even renewal r* = 1 − (revenue per order ÷ CAC). Since revenue per order divided by cost per order equals revenue over cost, r* = 1 − ROI exactly. Verified against all four channels to four decimal places.
  • Efficiency reduces to LTV ÷ CAC = ROI ÷ (1 − r), which is why relative channel ranking is invariant to r when the rate is assumed common across channels.
  • Crossover: Meta matches Google's efficiency when r_meta = 1 − (1 − r_google) × ROI_meta ÷ ROI_google. At Google 50% and Google ROI excluding week four, Meta requires 71.5%.
  • Assumed renewal rates used for the illustrative table (Meta 35%, Google 50%, Affiliate 30%, Organic 65%) are estimates from intent signals in the funnel, not measured values, and are labelled as such wherever they appear.
Corrections and exclusions
  • Organic week two: a transcription error in the source. Corrected before any aggregate was computed.
  • Google week four: trial to order fell from 29% to 10% while registrations and trial signups held flat at 3,500 and 2,030. A genuine demand shift would show at the steps above; an isolated collapse at the payment step is consistent with a tracking or checkout failure. Excluded from averages, flagged to engineering, and its effect on the aggregate disclosed rather than absorbed.
  • Meta spend response: Pearson correlation across six weeks between spend and registration to trial is -0.930, spend and trial to order is -0.872, spend and ROI is -0.770. Correlation between week number and conversion is -0.116, which is what rules out a simple time trend.
